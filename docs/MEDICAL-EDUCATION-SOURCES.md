# Education and real-world help boundaries

Med Quest uses simplified models for body-system literacy. Its demonstrations are not clinical instructions, injury assessments, medical timers, return-to-play tests or dosing guidance. The exact boot and journal disclaimer remains present.

The expanded brain and Emergency lessons were checked on October 1, 2026 against these primary sources:

- CDC HEADS UP, responding to a sports-related concussion: https://www.cdc.gov/heads-up/response/index.html . Suspected concussion means stopping sports participation; return requires healthcare-provider clearance. The game's brain quiz explicitly cannot clear a child to play.
- CDC HEADS UP, signs and symptoms: https://www.cdc.gov/heads-up/signs-symptoms/index.html . Danger signs need immediate emergency medical care; the game teaches reporting symptoms to a trusted adult, not diagnosis.
- American Red Cross, respiratory distress: https://www.redcross.org/take-a-class/resources/learn-first-aid/respiratory-distress-trouble-breathing . Serious trouble breathing needs urgent emergency help. Emergency mode teaches obtaining adult and emergency help, not airway manipulation.
- American Red Cross, child and baby first aid: https://www.redcross.org/take-a-class/first-aid/performing-first-aid/child-baby-first-aid . Serious emergencies require immediate emergency services and care appropriate to training. Med Quest does not qualify players to provide care.

Curriculum adaptation: S3's range task uses a healthy diagram marker, with an explicit instruction never to force a real painful muscle. S4's short pad and platelet demonstrations are game observations, not a real first-aid protocol or treatment duration. S7 explores a food token in a model and never prescribes manipulations for real abdominal pain. S9's short pause represents stopping play, not healing or clearance. Emergency stories make no real calls and direct learners to trusted adults and local emergency services; 911 is labeled as the US example.
